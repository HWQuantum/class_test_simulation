How much of the course should a test cover?

Each student answers each question correctly with a probability that depends on how strong they are and whether they revised that topic. The test assesses a random selection of topics. Change how much of the course is assessed and see what happens to the class average, the spread of grades, and how many students pass.

How the model works

Each topic gets a difficulty drawn from a normal distribution, centred so the course is neither harder nor easier overall. A student's chance on a topic starts at their p revised or p not revised, then is shifted by the topic's difficulty on the log-odds scale. Each question is an independent Bernoulli trial with that chance, and a student's score is the percentage they got right.

A test draws its topics at random without replacement. Coverage is rounded to a whole number of topics, so with 10 topics, 25% becomes 3 topics.

Two things make low-coverage tests noisier, and the test-length setting separates them. When the test is just every question from the chosen topics, low coverage also means fewer questions, which adds plain sampling noise. Switch to a fixed number of questions to see the effect of topic coverage on its own.

The within-class standard deviation and the between-test standard deviation answer different questions. The first is how spread out the grades are on one sitting. The second is how much the class average depends on which topics came up, which is the gamble a teacher takes when assessing a small part of the course.
